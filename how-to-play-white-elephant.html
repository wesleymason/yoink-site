How to play white elephant

A step-by-step guide to hosting one, from the invite to the last steal.

A good white elephant needs three things: a clear budget, a turn order nobody can dispute, and rules agreed before the first gift is opened. Here's the whole night, from invite to last steal.

Before the party

1. Pick a budget and say it twice

$20 to $30 is the sweet spot for most groups: enough that gifts are worth stealing, not so much that anyone feels burned. Put the budget in the invite and mention it again the week of. A gift that's wildly under or over the budget is the only thing that reliably sours the game.

2. Decide the house rules in advance

Steal limit, steal-backs, and whether player 1 gets a final swap. See the rules guide for what each one does. Put the rules on the invite so guests arrive knowing them.

3. Send a real invite

A group text works. Include the date, the budget, the rules, and whether gifts should be funny, nice, or themed. If you use an app like Yoink, the invite link carries all of that and guests can RSVP from it, so you know your head count without chasing anyone.

4. Have a few spare gifts

Someone always forgets. Two wrapped backups, bought within budget, mean a forgetful guest can still play instead of sitting out.

On the night

5. Collect the gifts out of sight

Have guests drop gifts on a table as they arrive, before anyone's watching, so no one knows whose is whose. Shuffle the pile once everyone's there.

6. Draw the order

Numbered slips in a bowl, a shuffled deck, or a tap in an app. Announce the full order once so people know roughly when they're up. Whoever is player 1 should hear that they get the final swap, if you play that way.

7. Read the rules out loud

Thirty seconds, before the first gift. "Open or steal. If you're stolen from, you go next. No stealing right back. Three steals and it's frozen. Player 1 swaps at the end." Agreeing now saves ten minutes of arguing later.

8. Run the turns

Call each player by name, loudly. When a gift is stolen, say who's up next and why. Keep a tally of steals per gift, because by the eighth turn nobody remembers. This is the job an app does best: it knows whose turn it is, how many times each gift has been stolen, and when one freezes.

9. Make steals an event

The steal is the whole game. Let the room react. If you play with duels, have the props ready: cups, cookies, a deck of cards. A thirty-second contest between the thief and the owner is the moment people talk about afterward.

10. Finish cleanly

After the last gift is opened, run the final swap if you use it, then call it. Take a photo of everyone holding what they ended up with. If someone truly hates their gift, a voluntary trade afterward is fine; it just isn't part of the game.

How long does it take?

Roughly three minutes per player with a steal limit, longer without. Ten people is about half an hour; twenty is an hour and should use a steal limit of two. Over twenty-five, split into two games.
